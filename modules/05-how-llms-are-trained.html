Module 05

How LLMs are Trained

⏱ ~13 min read

What you'll learn

  • The three big phases: pretraining, fine-tuning, and alignment.
  • What RLHF is, conceptually, and why it exists.
  • What a "knowledge cutoff" is and why it matters in practice.

Phase 1: Pretraining — learning language broadly

Training usually starts with pretraining. The model is shown an enormous body of text and, over and over, tries to predict the next token (Module 3). Each time it's wrong, its parameters are nudged slightly. Repeat this billions of times and the model gradually absorbs grammar, facts, writing styles, reasoning patterns, and a great deal of general world knowledge.

Pretraining is the expensive, time-consuming part — large data, lots of GPUs, significant cost and energy. The result is a "base model" that's good at predicting text but not yet shaped into a helpful assistant.

Phase 2: Fine-tuning — specializing the model

Fine-tuning continues training the base model on a smaller, more targeted dataset to adapt its behavior. You might fine-tune for a domain (legal, medical, support tickets), a style (concise, formal), or a format (always answer as JSON). It's far cheaper than pretraining because you're adjusting an already-capable model rather than starting from scratch.

IT analogy

Pretraining is like hiring someone with a broad general education. Fine-tuning is the onboarding and role-specific training that teaches them your team's tools and conventions. Same person, now tailored to the job.

Phase 3: Alignment — making it helpful and safe

A raw model that just predicts text isn't necessarily helpful, honest, or safe. Alignment steers it toward responses people actually want. The best-known technique is RLHF — Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback.

Conceptually, RLHF works like this:

  1. The model produces several candidate answers to many prompts.
  2. People rank which answers are better (more helpful, accurate, appropriate).
  3. Those human preferences are used to train the model to produce more of the kind of answers people preferred.

The effect is a model that follows instructions, declines harmful requests, and adopts a helpful tone. Alignment reduces bad behavior but doesn't make a model perfect — it still has the limitations we'll cover in Module 10.

Knowledge cutoff: the model's "as of" date

Because a model learns from a fixed snapshot of training data, it has a knowledge cutoff: a point in time after which it simply hasn't seen anything. Ask about an event, product, or patch released after the cutoff and the base model may not know — or worse, may confidently guess.

This has real consequences for IT:

  • Answers about fast-moving topics (new CVEs, recent software versions) can be stale.
  • The model often won't tell you it's out of date unless asked — it has no inherent sense of "today."
  • To stay current, tools pair the model with live data via web access or retrieval (Module 8, RAG). The base model's parameters alone are frozen at the cutoff.

Does using a model "train" it on your data?

Not automatically. Typing into a chatbot is inference, not training — your message doesn't instantly become part of the model. However, some providers may log your inputs and could use them to improve future models depending on their terms. That's a privacy and governance question (Module 11), separate from how training works.

Key takeaways

  • Pretraining teaches broad language from huge text; it's the costly phase.
  • Fine-tuning cheaply specializes a base model for a domain, style, or format.
  • Alignment (e.g. RLHF) uses human preferences to make models helpful and safe.
  • A knowledge cutoff means the base model can't know events after its training snapshot.
