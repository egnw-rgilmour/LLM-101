This is the module that matters most for IT. The technology is interesting, but the day-to-day risk is about where your data goes and who controls it.

The core risk: data leaves your control

When someone pastes text into a third-party AI tool, that data is transmitted to, and often processed and stored by, an outside provider. Depending on the provider's terms, it may be retained, logged, or used to improve their models. Once it leaves your boundary, you no longer fully control it.

The danger is that it feels harmless — it's just a chat box. But employees paste source code, customer records, contracts, credentials, incident details, and personal data without thinking of it as "sending data to a vendor." Treat every external AI tool as a third-party data processor, because that's what it is.

Golden rule

Don't paste anything into a third-party AI tool that you wouldn't email to an outside company. Secrets, regulated data (personal, health, financial), and confidential IP need explicit approval and an appropriate tool — not an ad-hoc consumer chatbot.

Shadow IT: AI's fast lane

Shadow IT is employees using tools without IT's knowledge or approval. AI supercharges it: countless free, browser-based tools, a genuine productivity boost, and a low barrier to entry mean staff adopt them faster than IT can vet them. A well-meaning employee summarizing a confidential document in a random web tool is a classic example.

Reducing shadow IT is mostly about giving people a sanctioned path:

  • Publish a clear, practical acceptable-use policy (see below).
  • Offer approved tools that are good enough that people don't go looking elsewhere.
  • Educate on the "why," not just the "no" — people follow rules they understand.

Cloud-API vs. self-hosted / local models

A central architectural choice with direct data-governance impact:

  • Cloud-API models. You send data to a provider's service and get responses back. Pros: powerful, scalable, little infrastructure to maintain, always up to date. Cons: data leaves your environment, so you depend entirely on the vendor's security, retention, and contractual commitments.
  • Self-hosted / local models. You run a model on your own servers (or even a workstation). Pros: data stays in-house, maximum control, can work fully offline. Cons: you own the infrastructure, cost, maintenance, and expertise; capable models need real hardware.

Neither is universally "more secure." A well-governed cloud service with a strong contract can be safer than a poorly run internal deployment. The right answer depends on data sensitivity, compliance obligations, budget, and in-house skills. Many organizations mix both: local/private for sensitive data, cloud for lower-risk tasks.

Logging, retention, and compliance

  • Logging & retention. If prompts and outputs are logged, that store can contain sensitive data — and becomes subject to breaches, legal discovery, and retention rules. Ask what's logged, where it's stored, for how long, and who can access it.
  • Training on your data. Will your inputs be used to train or improve the vendor's models? Many enterprise tiers let you opt out or contractually forbid it — confirm in writing, don't assume.
  • Data residency. Some regulations require data to stay in a region. Check where processing and storage physically happen.
  • Regulatory scope. Personal, health, financial, and contractual data carry obligations (e.g. privacy laws, sector rules) that don't disappear because "an AI did it." Loop in legal/compliance early.
  • Access & auditability. Who in the org can use the tool, with what data, and can you audit that usage?

IT analogy

Evaluating an AI vendor is the same discipline as evaluating any cloud SaaS that will hold your data: data flows, retention, encryption, access control, certifications, and contract terms. AI doesn't need a brand-new rulebook — it needs your existing third-party risk process applied consistently.

A vendor-neutral evaluation checklist

When an AI tool lands on your desk, ask:

  1. Data handling: What data does it receive, and is it stored, logged, or used for training? Can we opt out?
  2. Residency & retention: Where is data processed/stored, and for how long?
  3. Security posture: Encryption in transit/at rest, access controls, and recognized certifications or audits?
  4. Compliance fit: Does it meet the regulations that apply to the data it will touch?
  5. Contractual terms: What do the terms of service and data-processing agreement actually commit to?
  6. Access control & auditability: Can we manage who uses it and review usage?
  7. Deployment model: Cloud, app, or local — and does that match the data's sensitivity?
  8. Business need: What real problem does it solve, and is the value worth the risk and cost?

Start with an acceptable-use policy

A practical first governance step is a short, clear policy stating which categories of data may (and may not) be shared with which kinds of tools, and where to get an approved tool. Clear guardrails let people use AI productively and safely — far better than a blanket ban that just pushes usage into the shadows.

Key takeaways

  • The core risk is data leaving your control — treat every external AI tool as a third-party processor.
  • Don't paste anything you wouldn't email to an outside company.
  • Shadow IT spreads fast with AI; counter it with clear policy and good sanctioned tools.
  • Cloud vs. local is a data-governance trade-off, not a fixed winner.
  • Scrutinize logging, retention, training-use, residency, and compliance — and apply your existing vendor-risk process.
