Module 01

What is AI?

⏱ ~12 min read

What you'll learn

  • What people actually mean by "AI" — and why it's an umbrella term.
  • How AI, machine learning, deep learning, and generative AI nest together.
  • How to cut through buzzword confusion when someone says "it uses AI."

AI is a goal, not a single technology

"Artificial intelligence" is one of the oldest and broadest terms in computing. At its simplest, it means getting computers to do things that normally seem to require human intelligence — recognizing a face, understanding a sentence, recommending a movie, flagging a fraudulent transaction.

That's a goal, not a specific tool. A hand-written rules engine that decides whether to approve a loan is "AI" in the classic sense, and so is a modern chatbot. This is why "it uses AI" tells you almost nothing on its own — it's like a vendor saying "it uses software." The useful question is always: what does it actually do, and how?

The nested circles: AI ⊃ ML ⊃ deep learning

The cleanest way to keep the buzzwords straight is to picture nested circles, from broadest to narrowest:

  • Artificial Intelligence (AI) — the whole field. Any technique that makes a machine act "intelligently," including old-school hand-coded rules.
  • Machine Learning (ML) — a subset of AI where the system learns patterns from data instead of being given explicit rules. You show it many examples; it figures out the pattern.
  • Deep Learning — a subset of ML that uses neural networks with many layers. It's especially good at messy inputs like images, audio, and language.
  • Generative AI — a use of (usually) deep learning that creates new content: text, images, code, audio. Large Language Models live here.

IT analogy

Think of "AI" like "networking." Networking covers everything from a crossover cable to global routing. Saying a product "does networking" isn't useful until you know whether it's a switch, a firewall, or a VPN. "AI" works the same way: ask which specific capability is on offer.

Where the confusion comes from

Marketing blurs these lines on purpose. A few clarifications that help in real conversations:

  • Not all AI is generative. Fraud detection, demand forecasting, and recommendation engines are AI but don't generate new content.
  • Not all AI learns. Plenty of "AI" is still rules and logic written by people. That's fine — it's just not machine learning.
  • "AI" is not automatically smart or reliable. The label says nothing about accuracy. That has to be evaluated separately (we'll cover that throughout the course).

Key takeaways

  • AI is a broad goal: machines doing things that seem intelligent.
  • ML is AI that learns from data; deep learning is ML using many-layered neural networks.
  • Generative AI creates new content — LLMs are a prominent example.
  • "It uses AI" is a starting point for questions, not an answer.
