Categories of AI tools

The market is noisy, but most tools fall into a handful of recognizable categories. Knowing the category tells you roughly what a tool does and what questions to ask — without getting lost in brand names.

  • Chat assistants. General-purpose conversational tools for drafting, summarizing, explaining, and Q&A.
  • Coding assistants. Help write, explain, review, and debug code, often inside the editor or IDE.
  • Search & RAG products. Answer questions grounded in a body of documents (Module 8) — internal knowledge bases, support portals, research tools.
  • Image, audio & video generators. Create or edit media from text prompts (and increasingly transcription, voice, and translation).
  • Embedded AI features. AI baked into existing products — your help desk, office suite, security tooling, CRM. Often the way AI quietly enters the org.
  • Agents & automation platforms. Tools that take multi-step actions across systems (Module 9), with the extra safety considerations that implies.

API vs. app vs. local

The same underlying model can be delivered in very different packages. The delivery model shapes who it's for, how data flows, and what IT must manage.

  • API. A building block for developers. Your software sends prompts and receives responses programmatically, so you can embed AI into your own apps and workflows. Maximum flexibility; requires development.
  • App. A finished, ready-to-use product (web, desktop, or mobile) for end users. Fast to adopt; you take the product as-is and rely on the vendor's choices.
  • Local. A model running on your own hardware or device, so data never leaves your environment and it can work offline. Strongest data control; you own the infrastructure and upkeep (Module 11).

IT analogy

It mirrors how you consume other software: an API is like a library you build on, an app is like packaged SaaS you roll out, and local is like on-prem software you host yourself. Same trade-offs of flexibility, convenience, and control.

A vendor-neutral evaluation framework

When a new AI tool lands on IT's desk, resist judging it by hype, brand, or a slick demo. Run it through a consistent framework so you can compare tools fairly and protect your decision from marketing pressure:

  1. Problem fit. What specific, real problem does this solve, and is that a problem worth solving? Beware solutions hunting for a use.
  2. Reliability. Does it solve that problem dependably enough for the stakes involved? Test on your own realistic examples, not the vendor's curated ones.
  3. Data & security. What data does it touch, and how is that handled, stored, and protected? (Use the Module 11 checklist.) This can be a deal-breaker on its own.
  4. Deployment model. API, app, or local — and does that match the sensitivity of the data involved?
  5. Total cost. Pricing model (per-token, per-seat, subscription), plus integration, training, and maintenance — not just the sticker price.
  6. Integration & lock-in. How well does it fit your existing stack, and how hard is it to switch later?
  7. Support & maturity. Is the vendor stable, responsive, and clear about limitations and roadmap?

Why vendor-neutral matters

A consistent framework lets you compare tools apples-to-apples on what actually matters — capability, data handling, cost, and fit — instead of on who marketed hardest. The newest, biggest, or flashiest tool is not automatically the right one for your task.

Key takeaways

  • Most AI tools fit a few categories: chat, coding, search/RAG, media, embedded features, and agents.
  • API = developer building block; app = ready product; local = on your own hardware.
  • Evaluate on problem fit, reliability, data/security, deployment, cost, integration, and support.
  • Stay vendor-neutral: judge tools on substance, not hype.
