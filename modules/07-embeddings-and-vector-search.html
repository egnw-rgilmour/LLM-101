Embeddings & Vector Search

⏱ ~13 min read

What you'll learn

  • What an embedding is: turning meaning into numbers.
  • How semantic similarity and vector search work.
  • What a vector database is and how it differs from keyword search.

Turning meaning into numbers

An embedding is a list of numbers (a vector) that represents the meaning of a piece of text. A model converts text into these numbers so that pieces with similar meaning end up with similar numbers — that is, close together in a mathematical space. Real embeddings have hundreds or thousands of dimensions; you can't picture that directly, but the idea scales from a simple 2- or 3-number version.

The key property: "How do I reset my password?" and "I forgot my login" land near each other, even though they share almost no words. Meanwhile "What's the weather?" lands far away. Closeness encodes similarity of meaning, not spelling.

IT analogy

Think of embeddings as geocoordinates for meaning. Two shops on the same street have nearby coordinates; two unrelated shops in different cities are far apart. Embeddings give every phrase a "location" so you can measure how near in meaning any two phrases are.

Semantic vs. keyword search

Traditional keyword search matches literal words. Search "car won't start" and it looks for those exact words; it may miss a document that says "vehicle fails to crank." Semantic search compares embeddings instead, so it finds results by meaning — matching synonyms and paraphrases keyword search would miss.

Neither is strictly better for everything. Keyword search is precise for exact terms, error codes, or product IDs. Semantic search shines when users phrase things differently from the source text. Many real systems combine both.

Try it: semantic similarity

Pick a phrase. The list shows which other phrases are "most similar" by meaning, using small hand-authored illustrative vectors (3 numbers each). Real embeddings use many more dimensions learned from data — this just conveys the concept.

Notice how the IT-support phrases cluster together, the weather phrases cluster together, and the finance phrases cluster together — even when they share no words. That clustering by meaning is exactly what makes embeddings useful.

Vector databases

Once you've turned thousands or millions of documents into embeddings, you need somewhere to store them and a fast way to find the closest matches to a query. That's a vector database: it specializes in storing vectors and performing quick nearest-neighbor lookups — "show me the 5 stored items closest in meaning to this query."

The typical flow: embed your documents once and store them; at query time, embed the user's question and ask the vector database for the nearest stored items. This is the engine behind semantic search over an internal knowledge base — and the retrieval step in RAG, which is next.

Key takeaways

  • An embedding represents meaning as a vector of numbers; similar meanings sit close together.
  • Semantic search matches by meaning; keyword search matches literal words.
  • A vector database stores embeddings and finds nearest matches quickly.
  • This powers meaning-based search over your own documents — and underpins RAG.
